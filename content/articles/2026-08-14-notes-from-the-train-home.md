## 序：

——记于回家的高铁路上

好久没写东西了。

从每天早起、坐公交、上班，到下班回家，生活好像逐渐被固定进了一套循环里。直到今天准备离开杭州回家一趟，坐在高铁上回头看这一个多月，于是想动手写点东西，权当作这段时间的感受。

## 1.关于上班与上学

正式上班实习，是从 7 月 13 日期末考结束后的第二天，也就是 7 月 14 日开始的。到今天 8 月 14 日，满打满算刚好一个月。

如果要用一句话总结这一个月的感受，大概就是：**珍惜大学时光。**

只有真正踩进职场，才会明白能有一段不被频繁打扰、可以凭着纯粹的心气去做自己喜欢之事的时间，有多么奢侈。

其实作为实习生，上班本身倒没有想象中那么累。有任务的时候会忙一些，没任务的时候也挺轻松。自己主要负责的项目一直在往前推，过程中难免遇到困难、卡点，但一点点把问题解决掉之后，那种成就感还是蛮爽的。

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="787" height="1400" src="/resources/train-notes-2-office-desk.jpg" alt="办公室的工位，合着的笔记本电脑，背景是同事堆满东西的桌子" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

但职场最耗人的，其实是那种无形的“场域”。

哪怕手头的事情已经做完了，你也很难真正沉浸下来做自己的事。因为你知道，随时可能有人过来找你，随时可能冒出新的任务。于是潜意识一直保持着一种待命和警醒的状态。

活可能没有很多，可一天下来，脑子还是会莫名其妙地觉得累。

综合这一个月的上班体验，工作日晚上回去以后，我基本就是刷刷视频、看看直播，放松一下，然后准备睡觉。真正还能有精力继续做自己事情的晚上，屈指可数。

有时候也会想，为什么明明没干多少体力活，一到晚上却什么都不想做。现在多少有点明白了：工作消耗掉的，很多时候不只是时间，还有注意力，以及那种“我今天还想再折腾点什么”的心气。

写下这些，也算给自己留一个提醒。

可以允许自己下班后休息、娱乐、躺平一会儿，毕竟人也不是机器。

但也得小心，别一不留神就一直泡在下班后娱乐的温柔乡里了。

毕竟，我还有很多自己的事情想做。

上学最大的压力是什么？对我来说，大概就是期末考试。

上班最大的压力是什么？任务、项目，以及最后能不能按要求完成交付。

如果一定要比较，我个人还是觉得后者更有挑战性。

大学里的很多课，想听就认真听，不想听偶尔摸摸鱼也行；到了期末，甚至还能靠一两周集中突击速通高绩点。和真正进入职场之后相比，大学在很多时候确实称得上“象牙塔”。

我们实习生目前还没有 KPI 考核，压力已经算小很多了。

但仅仅是项目推进过程中遇到一个卡点，就足够让人压力山大。一个问题迟迟解决不了，进度卡在那里，你会很自然地开始想：会不会影响后面的交付？要不要问问别人？该怎么汇报？下一步到底怎么推进？

更别提那些真正背着 KPI、业绩和结果责任的正式员工了。

所以我觉得，实习一个很大的意义，就是让你提前接触真实的职场，把脑子里原本那些关于“上班”的滤镜一点点打碎。

当然，目前遇到的公司前辈们都很 nice，这点确实很幸运哈哈哈。

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-3-desk-figures.jpg" alt="办公桌上摆着的几个盲盒公仔，旁边放着一包零食" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

还有一个我觉得挺有意思的变化：**上班真的很锻炼口头表达能力。**

因为只要你在做项目，就一定绕不开汇报。

刚来的时候，我有时候知道自己想表达什么，真正说出口却会绕来绕去。一个月下来，我明显感觉自己的口头表达能力好了很多。

项目现在做到哪一步了？遇到了什么问题？为什么会卡？做过哪些尝试？接下来准备怎么解决？有没有什么地方需要别人协助？

这些东西你自己脑子里乱成一团是不行的。汇报的时候，你得在很短的时间里把信息整理清楚，让别人快速听懂你在说什么，甚至马上知道下一步该怎么做。

说到底，其实就是一句话：

**一定要先想清楚，想清楚了，才能说清楚。**

当然，如果让我在学校里听一下午水课，和在公司里推进一个真实项目之间选，我大概率还是会觉得后者更有意思。

因为项目有目标、有反馈，也有真实的问题需要解决。你能明显感受到一件事情在自己手里一点点往前推进，这种感觉还是挺爽的。

只是上班和上学相比，有一点我现在越来越确定：

**大学对一个人心力的保护，以及对时间的自由支配，是工作以后很难拥有的。**

上班可能更真实，更锻炼人；大学则给了你更多完整的时间、注意力和精力，可以拿来阅读、学习、折腾自己的兴趣，甚至单纯发发呆。

所以现在再看“珍惜大学时光”这句话，感觉确实和以前完全不一样了。

以前听到只觉得是一句老生常谈。

真正上了一个月班之后，多少有点听懂了。

## 2.无社恐一说

因为学校住宿申请不予通过，也是在杭州租房了，这里要感谢一位朋友推荐的房东，解了燃眉之急。

当时为了租房，我真的是各种问、各种联系：小红书上看房源、问价格、问转租期限、问能不能短租，还要和不同房东来回沟通。折腾下来，租没租到另说，社交能力倒是实打实被练出来了。

想想刚高三毕业那会儿，上 IP 课的时候，我连主动和陌生人说话都会紧张。现在已经能在台风天里很自然地走进小卖部，问老板能不能接点热水了——当然，最好还是先买点东西再开口哈哈哈。

所以现在我越来越觉得，很多所谓的“内向”“社恐”，其实都和接触得太少有关。你见的人多了，开口的次数多了，被拒绝几次、尴尬几次，慢慢也就习惯了。

很多能力，真的就是练出来的。

## 3.公共图书馆+星巴克

在杭州，我目前最喜欢的一种周末打开方式，就是：

上午去浙江省图书馆坐坐，顺便蹭点免费热水；下午再找一家星巴克，打开 MacBook，一坐就是一下午。

## 4.上海明日方舟嘉年华之旅

7月底第一次出发去上海，实际上这样一个人出行还是有点紧张的，但是到了上海后，按照自己的规划走，突然感觉也没什么嘛。

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-5-arknights-fes.jpg" alt="明日方舟 2026 嘉年华的主视觉打卡板，气球和花艺围着 HYPERGRYPH 立体字" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

很多时候紧张只是因为“从来没经历过”。第一次遇到，大脑没有预案，所以会慌；真正经历过一次以后，它知道大概会发生什么、遇到问题该怎么处理，下一次自然就淡定多了。

**见人，见事，见世界。**

这趟嘉年华之旅玩得很开心，对我来说也是一种完全全新的体验。

今天下午刚好看到万维钢老师提到“复利”这个概念，其中有一条叫“体验资本”，原文是：

> 【第七是体验资本，你做过什么难事、去过哪些地方、跟谁一起经历过什么。高质量的体验叙事会沉淀为判断力、叙事能力和身份认同。科学家早就知道 [8]，体验型消费比纯物质消费更能提升长期幸福感。】

看到这段话的时候，我一下子就想到了这趟上海之行。

一开始买嘉年华门票的时候，我其实还有点肉疼。花出去的可都是真金白银啊。

结果玩完以后，只想说一句：值。

光手机里就凭空多出来了140多张照片（bushi）。

以前的我总会觉得，有些事情看看图片、刷刷视频，好像也差不多。真正到了现场以后才发现，亲眼看到、亲耳听到、自己走进去的感觉，完全不一样。

更重要的是，那些照片背后都对应着一段真实发生过的经历。**你去过那里，看过那些东西，和那座城市真正产生过连接，这种体验最后都会变成你人生记忆的一部分。**

我第一次走在南京路步行街上的时候，就很明显地感受到了这一点。

你走在人群里，看着街道两边的建筑、店铺和来来往往的人，会慢慢感受到这座城市的气质。

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-6-nanjing-road.jpg" alt="夜晚的南京路步行街，人流、店铺招牌和巨大的曲面 LED 屏" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

上海给我的感觉和杭州非常不一样。

这种区别很难用一两个形容词准确讲出来，可当你真正走在街头，你就是能够感受到。

尤其是后来站在外滩附近，遥望东方明珠和对岸那片繁华的时候，我当时真的有一种很强烈的震撼。

文字其实很难完全还原那一刻的心境。

大概就是——

**我突然意识到，原来世界还可以这么大。**

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-7-the-bund.jpg" alt="外滩夜景，东方明珠和陆家嘴的天际线倒映在黄浦江上" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

可能有人看到这里会觉得有点夸张。

但在今年以前，我真正去过的城市，一个手掌都数得过来。

以前的我就是很传统的宅家型选手，没什么事基本不会主动出去，也不会特意去探索新的地方。

多走几个地方，多见几个人，当下可能看不到什么直接收益，却会慢慢沉淀进你的判断力、审美、谈吐、眼界，甚至你对“自己想过什么样的人生”的理解里。

我想，这大概就是万维钢老师所说的“体验资本”。

这趟之后，我最先变的其实是消费观。

以前我的消费观很简单：**开源节流**。

能少花就少花，能不花就不花，手里有点钱就想着拿去理财投资。

这个想法当然没什么问题，我现在依然觉得投资很重要。但去了一趟上海以后，我开始重新理解“钱到底是拿来干什么的”。

几千块钱放进理财里，一段时间以后可能给你带来几百块收益。

可如果其中一部分钱，能让你去一座从没去过的城市，让你第一次看到以前只在照片和视频里见过的东西，让你产生新的感受、新的判断，甚至改变你看世界的方式，那它带来的回报，很难单纯用收益率来计算。

与旅游不同的是，你不必追这座城市的热门景点，你完全可以慢悠悠地city walk，感受这座城市带给你的感受，我觉得这个才是最重要的。

走走街道，看看店铺，坐坐地铁，进一家咖啡馆，观察这里的人，感受这里一天是怎么运转的。

而且很有意思的是，当你看过的东西越来越多以后，赚钱的欲望反而会越来越强。

因为你开始知道，这个世界上还有太多地方想去，太多事情想体验，太多东西想看看。

赚更多的钱，然后去看看更大的世界。

## 5.关于AI

前前后后用 Claude Code、Codex 和其他 LLM，加起来大概也快百亿token了。

用到现在，最直接的感受依然是：**AI 确实牛逼。**

甚至有时候我会想，如果没有 AI，还会有现在的我吗？

这百亿 Token 里，我确实借助 AI 做出了不少以前根本想不到自己能做出来的东西。比如 Claude Code 教学手册、FreeGrid，以及现在实习中推进的一些职场项目。很多事情放在几年前，我可能连从哪里开始都不知道，现在却可以靠着 AI把它们做出来。

但用得越多，我对 AI 反而也越来越“祛魅”。

因为到最后你会发现，**AI 的上限，很大程度上取决于使用它的人。**

它根据你的输入生成输出，而你提供什么背景信息、怎么思考、如何判断结果好坏、接下来往哪个方向调整，这些东西依然掌握在人手里。

很多人喜欢把 AI 想象成“许愿机”：我说一句话，它就应该自动把我脑子里的东西完美实现出来。

至少以我目前的使用体验来看，这种情况基本不存在。

我做过的绝大多数复杂项目，最后都是我和 AI 一轮一轮磨出来的。

先让它做第一版，我看哪里有问题；再调整方向，补充信息；它继续生成，我继续判断；发现不对，再推翻重来。

反复迭代很多轮之后，最后才慢慢接近我真正想要的东西。

**思考能力、判断力，以及做决策的能力依然由人把控。**

当然，也可能单纯是我现在还太菜了（笑）。

自己输入的信息不够完整，问题拆得不够清楚，所以 AI 输出也跟不上，只能靠多调几轮弥补。

再往后 AI 如果继续进化，说不定真有一天强到很多事情压根不需要我操心了，这个也不完全排除。

但至少现在，我越来越觉得，真正厉害的人使用 AI，会获得非常夸张的放大效应。

**而 AI 对现实工作的冲击，我这次实习也算是亲眼看到了。**

最近很火的 FDE 岗位是一方面，更重要的是，企业内部确实已经开始真正用 AI 改造工作流。

“AI 提效”这件事情，它确实正在企业里真实发生。

所以至少以我现在的判断来看，未来职场里，**会使用 AI 很可能会逐渐变成一种基础职业素养。**

不过 AI 用得越多，我反而也越来越确定另一件事：

**人与人之间真实的交互，依然很难被替代。**

七八月份，我也线下见到了很多以前认识的人。

有曾经在树成林认识的网友，也有去年一起做运营的朋友，还有线上实习时认识的前辈等等。

以前隔着屏幕聊天的时候，其实已经觉得很熟了。

可真正在线下见到真人，坐在一起吃饭、聊天，那种感觉还是完全不一样。

  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-8-dinner-one.jpg" alt="和朋友吃饭：清蒸鱼、荷叶包、白灼虾摆了一桌" loading="lazy" />

这种体验，至少现在的 AI 给不了我。

AI 可以陪我聊很多东西，可以帮我写代码、做项目、梳理思路，甚至有时候比很多人更懂我到底想表达什么。

但当一个真实的人坐在你面前时，带给你的真实的感觉，依然挺有意思的。

## 6.关于异地实习与回家（8.15补）

8.15，在家待了一天，突然发现其实和以往放暑假待在家里也没什么区别。甚至到了下午，我已经有点想回杭州了，hhh。

坐在家里的书桌前，会突然生出一种“日子好像已经看到头了”的感觉：今天这样，明天大概也是这样，一天天重复下去。如果每天都只是机械地重复，生活没有新的东西进入，这样的生活绝对不是我想要的。

其实这次回家，更多还是因为想家了。虽然平时可以打视频电话，但隔着屏幕，终究代替不了真实见面的感觉。偶尔短暂地回家，陪陪父母，其实还是很开心的。

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-10-home-table.jpg" alt="家里的饭桌，黄鱼、茄子炒辣椒、凉拌黄瓜和一碗玉米羹" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

不过再想想，上班下班的两点一线，和待在家里重复的日子，好像也没有太大区别。

更重要的还是每天究竟在做什么。如果每天做的事情都差不多，没有新的东西进入，那么无论是实习还是待在家里，本质上都只是在重复。

重复本身其实无法避免，绝大多数人的生活都由大量重复构成。真正需要警惕的，是有一天自己已经习惯了这种重复。

写在这里，也是为了提醒自己：**有意识地给生活添加点新东西。**

## Prologue

—Written on the high-speed train home

It has been a long time since I wrote anything.

Wake up early, take the bus, go to work, come home. Life had quietly settled into a loop. It was only today, packing up to leave Hangzhou for a short trip home, sitting on the train and looking back on the past month, that I felt like writing something down—call it a record of how this stretch of time actually felt.

## 1. On Working and Studying

My internship began the day after my final exams ended on July 13—so, July 14. Today is August 14, which makes it exactly one month.

If I had to sum up the month in a single sentence, it would be this: **treasure your time at university.**

Only after you have actually stepped into a workplace do you understand what a luxury it is to have a stretch of time that nobody keeps interrupting, time you can spend on whatever you care about purely because you want to.

Honestly, as an intern, the work itself is less exhausting than I imagined. Busy when there are tasks, quite relaxed when there are none. The project I am responsible for keeps moving forward, and while there are inevitably obstacles along the way, working through them one at a time feels genuinely good.

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="787" height="1400" src="/resources/train-notes-2-office-desk.jpg" alt="My desk at the office, laptop closed, a colleague's cluttered desk behind it" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

What actually wears you down is the invisible "field" of an office.

Even when everything on your plate is finished, it is hard to sink into anything of your own. Because you know someone could walk over at any moment, and a new task could land at any moment. So some part of you stays permanently on standby, permanently alert.

The workload may not be heavy, but by the end of the day your head is inexplicably tired.

Across this whole month, my weekday evenings have gone roughly like this: scroll some videos, watch a stream, unwind, go to bed. The evenings when I still had the energy to work on my own things, I could count on one hand.

I used to wonder why I felt like doing nothing at night when I had barely done anything physical. Now I think I understand: what work consumes is often not just time but attention—and that particular appetite for going home and tinkering with something.

Writing this down is partly a note to myself.

It is fine to rest after work, to be entertained, to lie flat for a while. Nobody is a machine.

But it is worth being careful, too, not to drift into that comfortable after-work haze and simply stay there.

Because I still have a lot of my own things I want to do.

What was the biggest source of pressure at school? For me, final exams.

What is the biggest source of pressure at work? Tasks, projects, and whether you can deliver what was asked of you.

If I had to compare them, I find the second more demanding.

Plenty of university courses let you pay attention when you feel like it and drift when you don't; come finals, you can cram your way to a decent GPA in a week or two. Compared with actually entering a workplace, university really does deserve the name "ivory tower."

We interns are not measured against KPIs yet, so the pressure is far lighter than it could be.

And even so, one stuck point in a project is enough to weigh on you. A problem drags on, progress stalls, and your mind starts running: will this affect the delivery date? Should I ask someone? How do I report this? What is the actual next step?

Never mind the full-time employees who genuinely carry KPIs, targets, and responsibility for outcomes.

So I think one of the real values of an internship is that it puts you in front of a real workplace early, and chips away at whatever filter you had in your head about what "having a job" means.

Though I have been lucky—everyone senior to me here has been genuinely kind.

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-3-desk-figures.jpg" alt="A few blind-box figurines on an office desk, a snack packet beside them" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

One more change I find interesting: **working is excellent training for speaking clearly.**

Because if you are running a project, you cannot avoid reporting on it.

When I first arrived, there were times when I knew exactly what I meant and still talked in circles trying to say it. A month later, I can feel the difference.

Where is the project now? What went wrong? Why is it stuck? What have you already tried? How do you plan to fix it? Is there anywhere you need help?

You cannot answer those with a tangle in your head. When you report, you have to organize the information in a very short window so someone else can follow you immediately—ideally so they know the next move right away.

Which comes down to one line:

**You have to think it through first. Only then can you say it clearly.**

That said, given the choice between sitting through an afternoon of a filler lecture and pushing a real project forward at a company, I would probably still find the second more interesting.

A project has goals, feedback, and real problems to solve. You can feel something moving forward in your own hands, and that feels good.

But comparing work and school, one thing keeps getting clearer to me:

**The way university protects your mental energy, and the freedom it gives you over your own time, is very hard to have once you are working.**

Work may be more real and more formative; university gives you larger blocks of time, attention, and energy—for reading, learning, chasing your own interests, or simply staring into space.

So "treasure your time at university" reads completely differently to me now.

It used to sound like a platitude.

After one month of work, I think I finally hear it.

## 2. There Is No Such Thing as Social Anxiety

My application for university housing was rejected, so I ended up renting in Hangzhou. I owe thanks to a friend who introduced me to a landlord and got me out of a tight spot.

Finding that place meant asking about everything and contacting everyone: browsing listings on Xiaohongshu, asking about prices, sublet terms, whether short leases were possible, going back and forth with different landlords. Whether or not I found a room, my social skills got a real workout.

Back when I had just finished my last year of high school and was taking an IP course, I got nervous just starting a conversation with a stranger. Now I can walk into a corner shop during a typhoon and ask the owner for some hot water—though it is still better to buy something first before you ask.

So I am increasingly convinced that a lot of what gets called being "introverted" or "socially anxious" simply comes from not doing it enough. Meet enough people, open your mouth enough times, get turned down a few times, be awkward a few times, and it stops being a big deal.

A lot of abilities really are just practice.

## 4. A Trip to the Arknights Carnival in Shanghai

I went to Shanghai for the first time at the end of July. Traveling alone did make me a little nervous, but once I arrived and simply followed the plan I had made, it turned out to be nothing at all.

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-5-arknights-fes.jpg" alt="The main photo wall at the 2026 Arknights carnival, balloons and flowers around the HYPERGRYPH letters" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

A lot of nervousness is just the absence of precedent. The first time, your brain has no plan, so it panics. Once you have been through it, it knows roughly what happens and how to handle problems, and the next time you are calm.

**See people. See things. See the world.**

The carnival was a lot of fun, and for me it was an entirely new kind of experience.

This afternoon I happened to read Wan Weigang writing about compounding, including a category he calls "experience capital":

> Seventh is experience capital: the hard things you have done, the places you have been, the people you have been through something with. High-quality experience settles into judgment, narrative ability, and a sense of identity. Scientists have long known that experiential spending raises long-term well-being more than purely material spending does.

Reading that, I immediately thought of this trip.

When I first bought the carnival ticket, it stung a little. That was real money going out.

Afterward, I had one thing to say about it: worth it.

For one thing, 140-odd photos materialized on my phone out of nowhere.

I used to think that for some things, looking at pictures or scrolling videos was close enough. Being there in person taught me otherwise. Seeing it with your own eyes, hearing it with your own ears, walking into it yourself—it is not the same thing at all.

More importantly, every one of those photos corresponds to something that actually happened. **You were there, you saw those things, you made a real connection with that city, and eventually that experience becomes part of your memory of your own life.**

The first time I walked down Nanjing Road, I felt this very clearly.

You walk through the crowd, looking at the buildings and shops on either side and the people going past, and slowly you start to pick up the character of the city.

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-6-nanjing-road.jpg" alt="Nanjing Road at night: crowds, shopfronts, and an enormous curved LED screen" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

Shanghai feels very different from Hangzhou.

The difference is hard to pin down in an adjective or two, but when you are actually standing on the street, you can simply feel it.

Later, standing near the Bund and looking across at the Oriental Pearl Tower and all that brightness on the far bank, I felt something close to shock.

Words cannot really reconstruct what that moment felt like.

Roughly, it was this—

**It suddenly hit me that the world could be this large.**

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-7-the-bund.jpg" alt="The Bund at night, the Oriental Pearl Tower and the Lujiazui skyline reflected in the Huangpu River" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

Some readers may find that overblown.

But before this year, the number of cities I had genuinely visited fit on one hand.

I used to be the classic stay-at-home type. Without a reason, I would not go out, and I certainly would not go looking for new places.

Going to a few more places and meeting a few more people may show no direct return at the time, but it settles slowly into your judgment, your taste, the way you talk, the range of what you can see—even into your sense of what kind of life you want.

That, I think, is what Wan Weigang means by experience capital.

The first thing that changed after this trip was how I think about money.

My approach used to be simple: **earn more, spend less.**

Spend as little as possible, spend nothing if you can, and put whatever is left into investments.

There is nothing wrong with that, and I still believe investing matters. But after one trip to Shanghai, I started reconsidering what money is actually for.

A few thousand yuan in an investment account might return a few hundred after a while.

But if some portion of that money takes you to a city you have never seen, shows you something you had only encountered in photos and videos, produces new feelings and new judgments in you, or even changes the way you see the world—that return is hard to express as a percentage.

Unlike tourism, you do not have to chase a city's famous sights. You can simply take a slow city walk and let the place register on you, which I think matters more.

Walk the streets, look at the shops, ride the subway, sit down in a café, watch the people, get a feel for how a day here runs.

And here is what I find genuinely interesting: the more you have seen, the stronger your desire to earn money becomes.

Because you start to realize how many places there are still to go, how many things there are still to do, how much there is still to see.

Earn more. Then go see more of the world.

## 5. On AI

Between Claude Code, Codex, and other LLMs, I have probably run through the better part of ten billion tokens by now.

After all that, my most immediate reaction is still: **AI is genuinely remarkable.**

Sometimes I even wonder whether I would be who I am now without it.

Across those tokens, I have built things with AI that I would never have imagined myself building: a Claude Code handbook, FreeGrid, and some of the projects I am now pushing forward at work. A few years ago I would not have known where to start on most of them. Now I can actually get them done.

But the more I use it, the more disenchanted I become, too.

Because eventually you notice that **the ceiling of AI depends heavily on the person using it.**

It generates output from your input, and what background you provide, how you think, how you judge whether a result is any good, which direction you push next—all of that still sits with the human.

A lot of people imagine AI as a wish-granting machine: I say one sentence and it should perfectly realize whatever was in my head.

In my experience so far, that essentially never happens.

Nearly every complex project I have finished came out of round after round between me and the model.

Let it produce a first version; I look for what is wrong. Adjust the direction, add context. It generates again, I judge again. When something is off, we throw it out and start over.

After many rounds of that, it gradually converges on what I actually wanted.

**Thinking, judgment, and the ability to decide still belong to the person.**

Of course, it may just be that I am not very good at this yet.

My inputs are incomplete, I break the problem down poorly, so the output falls short and I have to make up the difference with extra rounds.

If AI keeps improving, there may well come a day when much of this no longer requires my attention. I would not rule it out.

But for now, I am increasingly convinced that when genuinely capable people use AI, the amplification is enormous.

**As for AI's impact on real work, this internship let me see it firsthand.**

The much-discussed FDE role is one part of it. More importantly, companies really have started rebuilding their workflows around AI.

"AI for efficiency" is not a slogan. It is actually happening inside companies.

So as far as I can judge right now, **knowing how to use AI will likely become a baseline professional skill.**

And yet the more I use it, the surer I am of something else:

**Real interaction between people is still very hard to replace.**

Over July and August I met a number of people offline whom I had known for a while.

Some I knew from the Shuchenglin community, some were friends I did operations work with last year, some were people I had met during a remote internship.

Chatting through a screen, I already felt close to them.

But actually meeting them in person—sitting down together, eating, talking—was not remotely the same.

AI can talk with me about almost anything, write code, build projects, untangle my thinking—and sometimes it understands what I am trying to say better than most people do.

But when a real person is sitting across from you, what that gives you is still its own thing.

## 6. On Interning Away From Home, and Going Back (added Aug 15)

August 15. One day at home, and I realized it is not really different from any other summer break at home. By the afternoon I was already a little eager to get back to Hangzhou.

Sitting at my desk at home, I felt something surface: the sense that I could already see the end of these days. Today looks like this, tomorrow will look about the same, and so on. If every day is only mechanical repetition, with nothing new entering, that is absolutely not the life I want.

The real reason I came back, though, was that I missed home. Video calls are fine, but a screen cannot replace being in the same room. Coming home briefly now and then, spending time with my parents, does make me happy.

<figure class="shot">
  <img width="1400" height="1050" src="/resources/train-notes-10-home-table.jpg" alt="The table at home: yellow croaker, aubergine with peppers, cold cucumber, and a bowl of corn soup" loading="lazy" />
</figure>

But thinking about it further, commuting between home and the office is not so different from repeating the same days at home.

What matters more is what you are actually doing each day. If the days are all roughly the same and nothing new comes in, then whether you are interning or sitting at home, it is fundamentally repetition either way.

Repetition itself is unavoidable; most people's lives are built largely out of it. What is worth watching for is the day you stop noticing it.

So I am writing this down as a reminder to myself: **put something new into your life on purpose.**
